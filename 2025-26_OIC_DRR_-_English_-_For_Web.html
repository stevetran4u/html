At a glance

This departmental results report details the Office of the information Commissioner’s actual accomplishments against the plans, priorities and expected results outlined in its 2025–26 Departmental Plan.

These results align with the Office of the Information Commissioner’s vision, mission, values, and strategies.

Key priorities

The Office of the Information Commissioner identified the following key priorities for 2025–26:

  • Investing in and supporting our resources: continue to advocate for a funding model that provides the OIC with the resources needed to operate and reflects the Commissioner’s status as an independent agent of Parliament
  • Innovating and transforming operations: provide tools to the OIC to consistently improve its annual performance by assigning and concluding investigations more rapidly and to excel in a hybrid work environment
  • Maintaining and enhancing the organization’s credibility: upholding the right of access to information through the OIC’s investigations and reports, continue to issue orders, as needed, and seek to ensure their enforcement through litigation where necessary. In the lead-up to the next review of the Access to Information Act, contribute to finding new ways to make information available through means other than access requests to provide relief to an overstretched system.

Summary of results

The following provides a summary of the results the department achieved in 2025–26 under its “core responsibility” (its main areas of activity).

Core responsibility: Government Transparency

Actual spending: $12,862,249

Actual human resources: 90

In fiscal year 2025–26, the OIC continued to enhance its operations to better serve the public. Efficient resource management, including optimizing office space, modernizing information management tools, and reallocating funds to bolster investigative capacity were strategic priorities for the reporting period. A major undertaking included transitioning to a new information management system, leading to a stronger culture of information, stewardship and collaboration between sectors.

Over the course of the year, 5,839 complaints were submitted to the OIC and 5,894 were concluded. Of the new complaints submitted, 67% were found to be admissible, ensuring that only complaints falling within the scope of the Commissioner’s mandate were assigned to investigators.

The Commissioner has the authority to cease investigating complaints when an investigation is no longer necessary. This most often occur when a complainant has received a response to their access request through the OIC’s intervention. This allows the OIC’s resources to focus on complaints where requesters are still awaiting access to the information they seek. The OIC also continued to make progress resolving the most complex complaints in its inventory allowing the OIC to begin 2026–27 with an inventory largely composed of complaints that were either under active investigation or had recently been submitted and were awaiting assignment to an investigator. Working on contemporary complaints is the most efficient use of resources for both the OIC and the institutions subject to the Act.

The volume of complaints submitted to the OIC varies annually. Although numerous factors—such as the level of resources dedicated to institutions’ access to information and privacy units—can affect complaint volume, the OIC does not have the ability to regulate or predict the number of complaints it will receive in any given year.

During 2025–26, the OIC found 3,900 complaints to be admissible and concluded 3,955 complaints through investigations, reducing its inventory by 55 complaints. As of March 31, 2026, the inventory stood at 2,155 active complaints, of which only 9% were submitted more than two years ago. This surpassed the Commissioner’s goal of having no more than 15% of active complaints be more than two years old.

For more information on the OIC’s work under government transparency, read the “Results – what we achieved” section of its departmental results report.

From the Commissioner

 

Caroline Maynard
Information Commissioner of Canada

The tabling of the Office of Information Commissioner’s (OIC) 2025–26 Departmental Results Report marks an important opportunity to reflect on the progress made during the first year of my renewed mandate and to assess the impact of the priorities set out in my 2025–26 Departmental Plan.

My reappointment in November 2024 provided continuity for the OIC and stability within the federal access to information system. In the year that followed, my Office remained focused on its core responsibilities—investigating complaints and supporting Parliament—while advancing key initiatives aimed at improving transparency, strengthening compliance, and enhancing operational efficiency.

Throughout 2025–26, we continued to promote a culture of “transparency by default” across federal institutions. This work included sustained engagement with public servants at all levels to reinforce their responsibilities under the Access to Information Act and to encourage the proactive disclosure of information. These efforts are essential to alleviating pressures on a system that continues to face significant challenges, including persistent delays and the over-application of exemptions and redactions.

At the same time, my Office maintained a strong focus on ensuring compliance with the Act. I exercised my full range of authorities, including issuing orders and, where necessary, pursuing legal recourse to uphold the right of access. We also completed a systemic investigation, first launched in 2024, into matters related to requesting and obtaining access to information concerning the ArriveCAN application at the Canada Boarder Services Agency. I issued a final report that included numerous recommendations to address the shortcomings I identified.

Over the past year, collaboration has been another key pillar of our work. Consistent with the commitments set out in my Departmental Plan, and in line with the joint resolution adopted with my provincial and territorial counterparts in late 2024, we continued to work with partners across jurisdictions to advance shared principles of transparency and accountability.

Looking ahead, the review of the Access to Information Act remains a critical opportunity to modernize Canada’s federal access regime. During 2025–26, my Office actively contributed to discussions on reform, advocating for legislative changes that reflect current technological realities, strengthen oversight, and ensure that the system is optimized to uphold the right of access to information.

The ongoing challenges observed in information management and the timely provision of access underscore the need for sustained attention, innovation, and commitment from all participants in the system. By maintaining our focus on transparency, compliance, collaboration, and modernization, my team and I will continue to uphold and advance the fundamental right of access to information for all Canadians.

Together, these efforts reaffirm the OIC’s essential role in promoting accountability and strengthening trust in Canada’s federal institutions.

Caroline Maynard
Information Commissioner of Canada

Details on results

The following section describes the results for Government Transparency in 2025–26 compared with the planned results set out in the OIC’s departmental plan for the year.

Canadians receive timely resolution of complaints on how federal institutions process access to information requests

Results achieved

Reduction in the OIC’s inventory

By the end of 2025–26, the Commissioner concluded numerous new complaints and most of the complaints that were more than two years old in the inventory. During 2025–26, the OIC Registry found 3,900 complaints to be admissible and concluded 1,939 complaints by finding them inadmissible or closing them for other reasons. In addition, 3,955 complaints were completed through investigations, contributing to a further reduction in the inventory.

Only 9% of the complaints in inventory had been submitted on or before March 31, 2024. Whereas investigating older complaints can often present numerous challenges due to the passage of time. Working on contemporary complaints is generally more efficient because the information regarding how the underlying access requests were processed is contemporary, readily available, and easier to verify.

Whenever possible, complaints are resolved through discussions between the parties, clarification of issues, or other means. This remains the most efficient and preferred approach to concluding complaints. Where it is not appropriate or successful, the Commissioner will exercise the full extent of her investigative powers. She will also issue orders, as needed, when complaints are well founded. During 2025–26, the Commissioner issued 340 orders, the majority of which related to delay complaints.

The OIC continues to publish final reports and orders in CANLII and in its public Decisions Database to shed light for complainants and institutions on the Commissioner's decisions and her interpretation and application of the Act. Increasingly, institutions and complainants refer to published decisions to support their arguments and negotiate whenever possible, a resolution to the issue at hand. 

Consistently high levels of litigation

The Commissioner's orders are legally binding, meaning that institutions must implement them unless they apply to the Federal Court for a review. Reviews under the Act are generally initiated by parties who are dissatisfied with the outcome of the Commissioner's investigation or with the subject matter of her orders. As a result, the Commissioner has limited control over the volume of incoming litigation.

The OIC handles all litigation internally, generating cost savings and efficiencies while benefiting from the specialized knowledge and experience of its Legal Services unit members in access to information law. However, during 2025–26, the OIC continued to experience consistently high levels of new court proceedings that required its involvement. Among those court proceedings, the Commissioner applied for a writ of mandamus against a head of an institution on one occasion because the institution had not taken the required action: they had neither implemented her order nor applied to the Federal Court for a review. In this instance, the application was discontinued upon the institution complying with the Commissioner’s order.

The majority of the reviews under the Act were applied for by institutions. A large proportion of these proceedings related to delay complaints, where the Commissioner had ordered the institution to respond to the access request at issue by a certain time. These proceedings are frequently discontinued when the institution ultimately responds to the access request but nonetheless generate significant amounts of work for the OIC Legal Services unit. In 2025–26, one application relating to a delay complaint was heard by the Federal Court. The Commissioner is currently awaiting a decision on that matter.

Of the reviews commenced under the Act, four such proceedings related to exemption complaints investigated by the OIC. While lower in number, these types of proceedings consume a large portion of the OIC Legal Services unit’s resources. Regardless of the outcomes, these types of reviews assist in the development of the body of law governing federal access to information requests.

Driving efficiency: continued progress

In 2025–26, all departmental performance results improved compared with the previous year, demonstrating continued progress across key performance indicators. These results are due in part to the OIC’s effective implementation of its Professional Development Program for Investigators in order to attract and retain a diverse and qualified workforce. The OIC recognizes that attracting and retaining a diverse workforce depends on fostering an inclusive work environment. A diverse workforce enhances the organization’s capacity to innovate and leverage talent, both now and in the future.

The Investigation Governance and Strategic Guidance unit, with the support of the Legal Services unit, also contributed to the efficient delivery of the investigations program by developing more tools and delivering training opportunities for investigators and Registry staff. The Legal Services unit provided continued support to the Commissioner by offering advice on a wide range of internal services and investigation-related issues, including investigations findings and reports, and institutions’ applications seeking the Commissioner’s approval to decline to act on access requests.

Enhancing the efficiency of the OIC’s operations has been a key priority since the outset of the Commissioner’s mandate. In 2025–26, significant progress continued—particularly in the timeliness of complaint assignments—driven by the implementation of new tools and streamlined processes.

Key risks

It is impossible to predict the number of complaints the OIC will receive each year, nor the number of applications for review before the court. Various factors may influence this volume, including the resources allocated to institutions’ access to information and privacy units. This unpredictability creates a major operational risk for the OIC. While funding and resources have remained stable, complaints fluctuate annually, and litigation has risen sharply since 2021–22, with the OIC handling all litigation internally. The OIC has been managing this risk by temporarily reallocating resources internally. Should the volume and complexity of litigation remain high, this will require a reassessment of permanent funding for the OIC’s legal services unit.

Despite its small size, the OIC remains responsible for ensuring that its IT services, infrastructure and support continue to meet operational needs in an evolving technology environment. In recent years, the OIC has modernized parts of its infrastructure by moving selected services to the cloud. This approach reduces the need to maintain the same level of on-premises servers, patching and infrastructure support, allowing existing IT resources to focus more on projects that create value for the organization. However, the transition also requires ongoing investment in cloud services, cybersecurity, software and hardware, at a time when technology costs continue to increase while funding remains static. The OIC’s mitigation strategy is to manage these pressures by prioritizing key modernization initiatives, using carry-forward funds where available, and spreading major technology projects over several years. Going forward, the OIC will continue to assess which additional services or infrastructure components should be moved to the cloud in order to increase efficiency, reduce operational burden and better manage long-term costs.

Although the OIC has been successful in reducing its inventory of complaints, Canadians are still facing longer than necessary delays in accessing the information to which they are entitled. As the Government and the OIC enter a period of greater fiscal restraint, the OIC does not want to lose the progress it has made. Although the OIC is committed to ensuring that financial resources are spent responsibly, there is the potential that limited financial resources will impact the public’s right of access. The OIC will continue to advocate for a funding model that reflects the Commissioner’s independence and provides the resources required to meet its mandate and that maintains Canadians’ confidence in the access to information system.

Related government priorities

This section highlights government priorities that are being addressed through this core responsibility.

Gender-based Analysis Plus

The OIC helps Canadians obtain access to information about government activities and decisions, including gender- based (GBA+) information. By giving Canadian’s access to information about government activities and decisions, the OIC has the potential to help the government meet all the goals in its Gender Results Framework. Due to privacy and confidentially requirements, the OIC does not collect or analyze GBA+ data from complainants. However, ensures equal treatment and prevents discrimination in any form.

In 2024–25, the OIC completed a complainant consultation about its program delivery, including the accessibility and inclusivity of its investigation processes. Complainants identified no significant barriers.

Artificial intelligence

As the Government has noted in its AI Strategy for the Federal Public Service 2025–2027, artificial intelligence (AI) provides enormous potential benefits through more efficient operations and enhanced service delivery for Canadians. Along with the benefits, AI also introduces new risks. The OIC is committed to the responsible use of AI, including addressing risks related to cost, security, privacy and the accuracy, reliability, and effective use of AI-generated outputs.

To support the adoption of AI, the OIC has released guidelines and introduced mandatory training on the appropriate use of generative AI tools to help employees understand their roles and responsibilities when using this technology, and to provide guidance on how to benefit from AI tools while mitigating risk. At present, OIC employees may use AI with non-sensitive data, and AI tools are being used across the OIC for tasks such as editing texts, generating images for presentations, summarizing publicly available information, brainstorming, research, learning, and translation of internal text.

Beyond these general applications, some areas of the organization have implemented specialized AI solutions to support their operations. Over the past two years, the OIC’s Linguistic Services unit has integrated an AI-based neural machine translation tool combined with a translation memory system to optimize its operations. The objective is to reduce the translation costs while maintaining the quality of translated content.

The OIC has also made significant investments in new IT and IM systems in recent years. The potential for greater use of AI tools has been an important consideration in shaping these efforts, with the aim of preparing applications and data to support broader AI adoption in the near future, particularly to automate time-consuming, repetitive tasks.

Progress on results

This section presents details on how the department performed to achieve results and meet targets for internal services.

Working in partnership with all sectors, Corporate Services enables the OIC to achieve its mandate by providing strategic advice, stewardship, and internal services that foster effective, efficient, and accountable operations. Consistent with this role, the sector has continued to prioritize investments in the Investigations Program, leading to a steady increase in resources over the last five years. This sustained focus has contributed to reducing both the size and the age of complaints in the OIC's inventory.

The spirit of efficiency and innovation that has guided Corporate Services in recent years continued in 2025–26 through ongoing investments by the Information Technology (IT) and Information Management (IM) units. These units worked together to migrate additional services to a cloud computing environment, with the aim of enabling OIC employees to work more efficiently while reducing the operational burden associated with maintaining internal infrastructure. In 2025–26, the IT and IM units also worked closely with the Investigations and Legal Services units to improve the OIC’s case management system used to manage complaints, investigations and legal activities. Building on this progress, the OIC will continue this modernization work in 2026–27, including planning for the transfer of this system solution to the cloud.

Another significant accomplishment in 2025–26 was the establishment of the Optimization Working Group. This initiative brings together employees from across the OIC to identify practical ways to enhance productivity and support more efficient operations. The working group has identified an initial set of priorities focused on targeted investments and solutions that can be implemented within the organization’s existing capacity and resources, while generating meaningful efficiencies for OIC employees.

Although the OIC was exempt from the Government’s Comprehensive Expenditure Review, the Finance unit worked with units across the OIC to undertake a review of spending to ensure that resources were used as efficiently and effectively as possible. Highlights in 2025-26 include reducing the OIC’s office space by approximately 20% while maintaining sufficient workspace capacity for all employees, and hiring additional IT staff to bring more internal expertise to the OIC which will reduce spending on consultants over time. The IT unit also reviewed their spending on software and found innovative ways to reduce or eliminate a number of licenses that will reduce ongoing spending.

In 2025–26, the Public Affairs unit supported the OIC’s mandate through strategic communications, stakeholder engagement, media relations, and outreach activities. The unit enhanced the visibility of the OIC’s work through digital communications, publications, and public reporting, helping to promote awareness of access to information rights and the OIC’s role.

The OIC also continued its leadership role within the federal, provincial, and territorial (FPT) access and privacy community. As Secretariat to the FPT Information and Privacy Commissioners in 2025–26, the OIC coordinated monthly virtual meetings and facilitated collaboration and information sharing among member jurisdictions. In addition, the OIC worked closely with the Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada to co-host and coordinate preparations for the 2026 annual FPT meeting in Ottawa, supporting the planning and delivery of a national conference for discussing emerging access to information and privacy issues.

Definitions

appropriation (crédit)

Any authority of Parliament to pay money out of the Consolidated Revenue Fund.

budgetary expenditures (dépenses budgétaires)

Operating and capital expenditures; transfer payments to other levels of government, departments or individuals; and payments to Crown corporations.

core responsibility (responsabilité essentielle)

An enduring function or role of a department. The departmental results listed for a core responsibility reflect the outcomes that the department seeks to influence or achieve.

Departmental Plan (plan ministériel)

A report that outlines the anticipated activities and expected performance of an appropriated department over a three-year period. Departmental Plans are usually tabled in Parliament in spring.

departmental priority (priorité)

A plan, project or activity that a department focuses and reports on during a specific planning period. Priorities represent the most important things to be done or those to be addressed first to help achieve the desired departmental results.

departmental result (résultat ministériel)

A high-level outcome related to the core responsibilities of a department.

departmental result indicator (indicateur de résultat ministériel)

A quantitative or qualitative measure that assesses progress toward a departmental result.

departmental results framework (cadre ministériel des résultats)

A framework that connects the department’s core responsibilities to its departmental results and departmental result indicators.

Departmental Results Report (rapport sur les résultats ministériels)

A report outlining a department’s accomplishments against the plans, priorities and expected results set out in the corresponding Departmental Plan.

Full-time equivalent (équivalent temps plein)

Measures the person years in a departmental budget. An employee’s scheduled hours per week divided by the employer’s hours for a full-time workweek calculates a full-time equivalent. For example, an employee who works 20 hours in a 40-hour standard workweek represents a 0.5 full-time equivalent.

Gender-based Analysis Plus (GBA Plus) (analyse comparative entre les sexes plus [ACS Plus])

An analytical tool that helps to understand the ways diverse individuals experience policies, programs and other initiatives. Applying GBA Plus to policies, programs and other initiatives helps to identify the different needs of the people affected, the ways to be more responsive and inclusive, and the methods to anticipate and mitigate potential barriers to accessing or benefitting from the initiative. GBA Plus goes beyond biological (sex) and socio-cultural (gender) differences to consider other factors, such as age, disability, education, ethnicity, economic status, geography (including rurality), language, race, religion, and sexual orientation.

government priorities (priorités pangouvernementales)

For the purpose of the 2025–26 Departmental Results Report, government priorities are the high-level themes outlining the government’s agenda as announced in the 2025 Speech from the Throne.

horizontal initiative (initiative horizontale)

A program, project or other initiative where two or more federal departments receive funding to work collaboratively on a shared outcome usually linked to a government priority, and where the ministers involved agree to designate it as horizontal. Specific reporting requirements apply, including that the lead department must report on combined expenditures and results.

Indigenous business (entreprise autochtones)

Requirements for verifying Indigenous businesses for the purposes of the departmental result report are available through the Indigenous Services Canada Mandatory minimum 5% Indigenous procurement target website.

non-budgetary expenditures (dépenses non budgétaires)

Net outlays and receipts related to loans, investments and advances, which change the composition of the financial assets of the Government of Canada.

performance (rendement)

What a department did with its resources to achieve its results, how well those results compare to what the department intended to achieve, and how well lessons learned have been identified.

performance indicator (indicateur de rendement)

A qualitative or quantitative measure that assesses progress toward a departmental-level or program-level result, or the expected outputs or outcomes of a program, policy or initiative.

plan (plan)

The articulation of strategic choices, which provides information on how a department intends to achieve its priorities and associated results. Generally, a plan will explain the logic behind the strategies chosen and tend to focus on actions that lead to the expected result.

planned spending (dépenses prévues)

For Departmental Plans and Departmental Results Reports, planned spending refers to the amounts presented in Main Estimates. Departments must determine their planned spending and be able to defend the financial numbers presented in their Departmental Plans and Departmental Results Reports.

program (programme)

An Individual, group, or combination of services and activities managed together within a department and focused on a specific set of outputs, outcomes or service levels.

program inventory (répertoire des programmes)

A listing that identifies all the department’s programs and the resources that contribute to delivering on the department’s core responsibilities and achieving its results.

result (résultat)

An outcome or output related to the activities of a department, policy, program or initiative.

statutory expenditures (dépenses législatives)

Spending approved through legislation passed in Parliament, other than appropriation acts. The legislation sets out the purpose and the terms and conditions of the expenditures.

target (cible)

A quantitative or qualitative, measurable goal that a department, program or initiative plans to achieve within a specified time period.

voted expenditures (dépenses votées)

Spending approved annually through an appropriation act passed in Parliament. The vote also outlines the conditions that govern the spending.

Gender-based Analysis Plus

Section 1: institutional GBA Plus governance and capacity

Governance

The OIC’s implementation of the GBA+ is supported by a strong organizational governance structure. The structure provides for direct reporting from the Equity, Diversity and Inclusion committee to the organizational head.  The OIC covers full spectrum of GBA+ categories, and does not have a specific target group.

Capacity

The OIC will further strengthen organizational measures rooted in the GBA Plus framework, with a strategic focus on enhancing mental health support, accommodation practices, and fostering a deeply inclusive culture. These efforts are directly informed by recent employee survey insights, ensuring that initiatives are responsive to staff needs and conducive to a supportive workplace environment.

Section 2: gender and diversity impacts, by program

Core responsibility: Government Transparency

Program name: Canadians receive timely resolution of complaints on how federal institutions process access to information requests.

Program goals: By giving Canadian’s access to information about government activities and decisions, the OIC has the potential to help the government meet all of the goals in its Gender Results Framework. As complainants remain anonymous, the privacy requirements hamper GBA+ data collection efforts to some extent, however, they ensure equal treatment and prevent discrimination in any form.

Target population: All Canadians

Distribution of benefits: The Access to Information program benefits all Canadians equally.

Key program impacts on gender and diversity: They key impact of the program is ensuring all Canadians receive timely resolution of complaints on how federal institutions process access to information requests.
